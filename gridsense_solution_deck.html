Explain the Challenge
The Situation

The Campus That Wastes Energy After Hours

Walk through any Indian engineering campus at 10:00 PM. The labs are empty, but the lights are blazing. Projectors hum in vacant lecture halls. In one department, five air conditioners run at full blast, cooling an empty seminar room. The library keeps three full floors lit and chilled for a handful of students clustered in one corner.

The administration knows electricity bills are through the roof. But they do not know where the waste is happening, why it is happening, or what to do about it without disrupting academic life or making someone's job harder.

The Core Conflict

Campus energy systems run on static timers and manual switches designed in the 1990s. Facility managers cannot simply cut power without risking safety or disrupting research. Manual guard patrols are inconsistent and happen hours too late. The administration has bulk utility data but no actionable insight from it.

The fundamental mismatch: Energy infrastructure is static and scheduled, but human presence is dynamic and unpredictable. GridSense bridges this gap by making every energy decision data-driven, occupancy-aware, and contextually intelligent.

What Makes This Hard to Solve

  • Activity is fragmented across large buildings, so entire cooling loops and transformer circuits remain energized for 2 or 3 students scattered across a 5-floor block.
  • Turning things off automatically risks disrupting active students or faculty who stayed late without notification.
  • Administrators receive raw monthly kWh bills, not actionable per-room or per-hour breakdowns.
  • Installing individual sensors in every room across a 50-acre campus is prohibitively expensive and takes months.
02
What is the Problem
Problem Definition

The Decoupling of Energy and Occupancy

In a standard Indian B.Tech campus, the physical layout creates five distinct energy waste zones. Each zone has its own waste mechanism, and each contributes differently to the monthly electricity bill.

A
Academic Blocks: The Empty-Room Full-Load Problem
Block A (Admin), Block B (Lecture Halls), Block C (Labs)

Block A has one class at 7:00 PM while Block C has five. The entire Block A corridor lighting system (80 metres), all HVAC units, and elevator standby draws remain active for a single class. Simultaneously, lecture halls run air conditioning at 18 to 20 degrees C with 10 students for a class scheduled for 60.

Root Cause: Static allocation, no consolidation intelligence
B
Computer Labs: The Phantom PC Drain
60 to 120 PCs per lab across CSE and ECE departments

A 60-PC computer lab left in idle desktop mode draws between 80 and 120 Watts per machine. After 6:00 PM, with no active users, all 60 machines and their UPS inverters continue drawing power. The dedicated split AC runs to cool the heat these idle machines generate, compounding the waste.

Root Cause: No idle detection or scheduled shutdown policy
C
Hostels: Morning kVA Spike and 24-Hour Utility Losses
Hostel wing geysers, overhead pumps, and water coolers

Between 6:00 AM and 8:30 AM, all hostel wing geysers (2 to 3 kW each) fire simultaneously, pushing the campus above its contracted Maximum Demand (kVA) threshold. The state electricity board charges heavy penalty tariffs for every kVA above the MD contract threshold. Meanwhile, 15 commercial water coolers across all blocks run 24-hour active refrigeration, chilling water at 3:00 AM in locked, empty buildings.

Root Cause: Static timers, no demand management, no occupancy interlocking
D
Infrastructure: Elevators and WiFi Running at Full Capacity 24/7
4-lift elevator bank, 150 access points campus-wide

All 4 elevators in the academic block remain active with full cabin lighting, ventilation fans, and motor drives on standby through the night. 150 enterprise WiFi access points draw 15 to 30 Watts each via PoE, running their full 5 GHz radios constantly, even during the 11:00 PM to 6:00 AM window when academic blocks are locked.

Root Cause: No time-aware or occupancy-aware infrastructure scheduling
03
Gaps and Challenges
Why Existing Systems Fail

The Four Gaps No Current Solution Addresses

1
Gap 1: The Raw Data Trap

Facility managers receive end-of-month bulk utility bills or unmanageable 15-minute interval smart meter spreadsheets. They are told the campus consumed 48,000 kWh in October. This information is completely useless for action. Knowing kWh spiked is useless without knowing which room, which floor, and whether any person was present. No existing campus tool connects power draw data to real-time occupancy context.

What is needed: Decision-ready insights. "Block B Seminar Hall 2 wasted 42 kWh on Wednesday evening with zero registered occupants" is 1,000 times more useful than a raw kWh total.
2
Gap 2: The Hardware Cost Barrier

Every existing "smart campus" or "building management system" proposal begins with a full IoT sensor retrofit: PIR motion sensors in each classroom, occupancy sensors per HVAC unit, door contact sensors per lab. For a campus with 200 rooms, 60 lab PCs, 4 elevator shafts, and 150 APs, the hardware procurement, installation, and commissioning cost runs into tens of lakhs, with an 18 to 24 month deployment timeline. No hackathon project or first-phase pilot survives this.

What is needed: A solution that extracts maximum intelligence from infrastructure the campus already owns: its enterprise WiFi controller, its ERP timetable database, and its main smart meter.
3
Gap 3: The All-or-Nothing Dilemma

Existing campus energy policies are binary: either power stays on all night, or someone manually switches it off. Abrupt automated shutoffs anger professors and disrupt students working late on assignments. Pure manual policies ("please switch off lights before leaving") have a documented compliance rate below 30% in Indian college settings due to apathy and absent enforcement.

What is needed: A graduated, 5-tier intervention system that starts with gentle notifications, escalates through automated setpoint adjustments, and only reaches full isolation as a last resort on definitively empty zones.
4
Gap 4: Spatial Fragmentation is Invisible

No current system understands that having 1 class in Block A and 5 in Block C means Block A should be shut down and that lone class relocated. Spatial fragmentation is the single largest driver of after-hours energy waste in Indian campuses, yet it is entirely invisible to conventional Building Management Systems that think at the room level, not the wing or block level.

What is needed: A spatial consolidation engine that thinks at the building, wing, floor, and corridor zone level simultaneously and can re-route academic schedules to cluster activity and isolate empty infrastructure.
04
Solution and Architecture
GridSense Platform

An Intelligent Weighted Scoring Engine Across 8 Optimization Modules

Core Principle: GridSense does not treat all inputs as equal. Every data signal entering the system carries a pre-calibrated weight. The weighted combination of all signals produces a single Waste Index Score (WIS, 0 to 100) per zone per 15-minute cycle. The higher the score, the more urgent and aggressive the automated intervention.

The GridSense Scoring Engine

All inputs are grouped into 4 sub-scores, each carrying a weight based on reliability, impact, and cost of error.

Input Category Key Signals Sub-Score Weight Why This Weight
Occupancy WiFi client count, ERP timetable status, RFID entry logs 0.60 Occupancy is the single strongest predictor of wasted energy. An empty room should never run at full power.
Climate Outdoor temperature, daylight lux level, indoor temperature, humidity 0.23 Climate determines how much energy is appropriate for a given occupancy, not whether energy should run at all.
Energy Draw Smart sub-meter kW reading, deviation from occupancy baseline, MD proximity 0.21 Confirms whether the zone is actually overconsumming relative to its occupancy-adjusted expected baseline.
Schedule Time of day, day type (holiday/weekday), active override flag 0.12 Provides temporal context and prevents false positives during officially scheduled but sparsely attended events.
WIS = (Occupancy_Score x 0.60) + (Climate_Score x 0.23) + (Energy_Score x 0.21) + (Schedule_Score x 0.12)

Each sub-score is normalized on a 0 to 10 scale where 0 = ideal condition, 10 = maximum waste condition.
Final WIS range: 0 (perfectly efficient) to 100 (critical waste, full isolation required).

8 Optimization Modules: Ranked by Energy Impact

A
Bio-Climatic Classroom Optimization and Daylight-First Scheduling
Priority Rank 1 / Estimated Savings: 40% to 55% of total classroom energy
A1: Daylight-First Room Allocation

Each classroom is assigned a Daylight Autonomy Score based on its floor, window-to-wall ratio, and cardinal sun orientation. Morning sessions (8:30 AM to 12:30 PM) are prioritized into east and north-facing classrooms where natural daylight exceeds 300 lux, allowing ceiling lights to remain completely off. Afternoon sessions avoid unshaded west-facing rooms that absorb direct solar heat gain. Saves 60% of daytime artificial lighting usage through scheduling alone, with zero hardware.

A2: Occupancy-Scaled Air Conditioning

Each student radiates approximately 100 Watts of body heat. A class of 60 generates 6 kW of internal heat; a class of 10 generates only 1.2 kW. The system reads live ERP headcount and cross-references outdoor weather API data every 15 minutes. Setpoints adjust dynamically: 24 degrees C for full classes above 32 degrees C outdoor temperature, 26 degrees C for partial attendance in moderate weather, and full AC shutoff with fans only during mild or rainy weather below 24 degrees C. At 15 minutes before class end, the AC compressor shuts off and the blower circulates residual cold air. Saves 30% to 45% of HVAC power per classroom.

A3: Zoned Window-Row vs Interior-Row Lighting

Ceiling lights are split into two independently controlled zones. Zone A (window-adjacent rows) is locked off whenever outdoor ambient light exceeds 300 lux. Zone B (interior rows and blackboard strip) activates only when the room is occupied. Saves 50% of classroom lighting during daytime hours in perimeter classrooms.

A4: Smartboard and Audio-Visual Auto-Sleep

If no touch interaction, HDMI signal, or wireless screen-casting is detected for 7 continuous minutes, the smartboard drops to under 3 Watts sleep mode. At the precise class-end timestamp, a network command (IP/RS232/CEC) forces complete shutdown of the smartboard, audio amplifier, and projector relay. Eliminates 90% of AV standby waste.

60% lighting reduction 30-45% HVAC cut 90% AV standby eliminated
B
Spatial Building Consolidation and Wing Shutdown
Priority Rank 2 / Estimated Savings: 35% to 50% of after-hours building infrastructure energy

The consolidation algorithm runs at 4:00 PM daily for after-hours planning. If Block A has 1 scheduled class and Block C already has 5, the lone class is relocated to Block C (subject to room capacity, equipment availability, and accessibility constraints). The entire Block A wing is then isolated at the main Distribution Board. Corridor lights are split into 3 sequential relay zones. Only the zone containing active classrooms is illuminated, eliminating 50 to 80 metres of empty hallway lighting. Advance notice is dispatched via WhatsApp bot or ERP push notification to the class representative and professor with sufficient time to adjust.

Entire wing shutdown Zoned corridor lighting ERP-verified constraint-aware
C
Computer Laboratory Intelligent Power Management
Priority Rank 3 / Estimated Savings: 28% to 40% of academic IT block energy

A lightweight background agent deployed on every lab PC (Windows or Ubuntu OS image) detects keyboard and mouse inactivity. After 15 minutes of no user input, the PC drops to S3 Sleep mode, drawing under 2 Watts instead of 80 to 120 Watts. At 6:00 PM, a central network broadcast script gracefully shuts down every unlocked, unattended machine across all labs. Wake-on-LAN (WoL) allows the lab administrator to power all machines simultaneously the next morning with a single click. No smart plugs or additional hardware are required. Turning off 60 PCs also eliminates 6 kW of internal heat generation, allowing the dedicated split AC to shut down completely.

Zero hardware cost Network broadcast shutdown WoL instant morning restore
D
Dynamic Elevator Staging with Mandatory 2-Lift Safety Floor
Priority Rank 4 / Estimated Savings: 6,600 kWh per year per 4-lift bank (INR 59,400 annually)

The system never drops below 2 active lifts at any time, ensuring emergency evacuation capability, accessibility compliance, and mechanical redundancy. The dispatch matrix operates across 3 tiers based on timetable data:

Deenergizing 2 lifts during 15 after-hours hours saves 22 kWh per day (rounded up conservatively at 0.6 kW standby per inactive lift plus 20 saved round-trip cycles at 8 kW running load).

E
Hostel Block Geyser Staggering and Solar-Aligned Water Pumping
Priority Rank 5 / Estimated Savings: 25% to 35% of hostel peak demand costs and full avoidance of utility kVA penalty tariffs

All hostel wing geysers (2 to 3 kW each) fire simultaneously at 6:00 AM, pushing the campus above its contracted Maximum Demand (kVA) threshold and triggering penalty charges from the state electricity board. GridSense staggers geyser circuits wing-by-wing (Wing 1: 5:30 to 6:30 AM, Wing 2: 6:30 to 7:30 AM) using simple programmable contactors. Insulated water tanks retain heat for over 2 hours, so students in Wing 2 experience no reduction in hot water comfort. Overhead water pump schedules are shifted from the morning grid-peak window to the campus solar generation peak (11:00 AM to 2:00 PM).

Zero kVA penalty charges Solar-window pump alignment Zero student comfort impact
F
Campus Water Cooler and Cold-Chain Compressor Interlocking
Priority Rank 6 / Estimated Savings: 1,620 kWh per month (INR 14,580 per month)

Approximately 15 commercial water coolers (1.5 kW compressor each) across all 3 academic blocks run active refrigeration 24 hours per day, including through the night when buildings are completely locked. Smart relay timers cut power to all cooler circuits at 7:00 PM and restore them at 7:00 AM on weekdays. Coolers are fully off on Sundays and declared holidays. Insulated stainless cooling tanks retain chill for over 2 hours after shutdown, so late-staying students still receive cold water. In the cafeteria, temperature probe sensors inside cold storage optimize compressor run cycles based on off-hours door seal integrity, preventing frost buildup and unnecessary short-cycling. Eliminating 12 hours of idle compressor runtime across 15 units saves 54 kWh per day (rounded up) and 1,620 kWh per month.

1,620 kWh saved/month INR 14,580/month savings Zero inconvenience to users
G
Time-of-Day Heavy Infrastructure Load Shifting
Priority Rank 7 / Estimated Savings: 20% to 30% reduction in peak-tariff electricity expenditure

Indian state electricity boards (BESCOM, MSEDCL, TANGEDCO, TSSPDCL) charge institutional consumers 20% to 40% higher tariff per unit during peak hours (6:00 PM to 10:00 PM). Non-critical high-draw motors (Sewage Treatment Plant aeration pumps, overhead water distribution pumps, central RO water purification plants) are locked out automatically between 6:00 PM and 10:00 PM via contactor interlocks and shifted to off-peak windows. Solar-surplus hours (11:00 AM to 2:00 PM) are preferred for all heavy pumping and treatment cycles, directly offsetting grid draw with captive renewable generation and flattening the campus peak demand curve to prevent Maximum Demand penalty charges.

20-30% peak tariff reduction Solar-window alignment MD penalty elimination
H
Enterprise WiFi Access Point Scheduled Power Reduction
Priority Rank 8 / Estimated Savings: 1,800 to 2,700 kWh per month across a 150-AP campus

Each PoE access point draws 15 to 30 Watts continuously. Across a 150-AP campus, that is 3 to 5 kW running 24 hours per day regardless of whether any student is on campus. Between 11:00 PM and 6:00 AM in academic blocks, the central network switch controller (via PoE schedule) disables secondary 5 GHz radios and drops APs to low-power 2.4 GHz IoT-only beaconing mode (under 5 Watts per unit). Hostel APs remain at full power. This saves approximately 6 to 8 hours of full-load PoE draw per AP per night across all academic block access points.

  • Enterprise WiFi controller with SNMP API (Aruba, Cisco, UniFi)
  • ERP system with timetable and attendance data
  • Main smart meter at the campus incomer panel
  • Existing network infrastructure for LAN communication
  • Academic calendar and holiday data
Zero new wiring for Phase 1
What GridSense Adds
  • Python FastAPI scoring engine connecting all data sources
  • Supabase PostgreSQL database for telemetry and audit logs
  • Branch-level smart sub-meters (Phase 2)
  • DB contactor relays on wing and zone circuits (Phase 2)
  • WhatsApp/Telegram notification bot (zero cost)
  • Real-time campus energy dashboard (web-based)
Phase 1 is 100% software-only
Minimum Viable Data Principle: GridSense is designed to deliver actionable value from Day 1 using only the WiFi controller API and ERP timetable data, both of which every modern Indian engineering college already operates. The system incrementally incorporates more data sources as Phase 2 hardware is commissioned, improving its WIS accuracy with each addition.

Scalability and Replication

  • GridSense is not built for one campus. The scoring engine, weight system, and intervention ladder are fully parameterizable. Any campus with a WiFi controller API and ERP system can be onboarded within 2 weeks.
  • Weights can be tuned per campus based on its specific building layout, tariff structure, and occupancy patterns. A campus in Chennai with high summer temperatures will naturally see a higher Climate sub-score weight than a campus in Shimla.
  • The adaptive learning engine ensures that GridSense becomes more accurate with every 30-day recalibration cycle, converging on the ideal weight profile for each campus over time.
Pilot Deployment Proposal: A single academic block with one WiFi controller integration and one branch smart meter is sufficient to validate the WIS engine and demonstrate savings within the first week. Total pilot cost: under INR 50,000 for hardware, with payback in under 2 months from the savings on that single block alone.
